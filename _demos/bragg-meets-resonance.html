A uniform bar carries a mass-spring resonator in every cell. The resonator mass stays fixed while its stiffness runs from zero to infinity. Choose a stiffness and a frequency, and see how the local-resonance gap moves through the Bragg gaps and merges with each one in turn.

Two kinds of gap in one bar

A periodic array of resonators can stop waves in two ways. Near the resonance frequency \(\omega_r=\sqrt{k/m}\), each resonator moves against the bar and acts as a large or negative mass, which opens a local-resonance gap. Because the resonators repeat every cell, they also scatter waves coherently and open Bragg gaps near \(\omega=n\,\omega_B\), where \(\omega_B=\pi c/a\) is the frequency at which half a wavelength fits in one cell. With \(k=0\) the resonators are detached and the bar has no gaps. As \(k\) grows, the local-resonance gap rises from zero frequency and the Bragg gaps widen. The frequency \(n\,\omega_B\) is normally a band edge whose standing wave has a node at every resonator, but when \(\omega_r=n\,\omega_B\) it falls inside a gap, and the local-resonance gap and Bragg gap \(n\) merge into one wide gap (references 1 and 2). As \(k\to\infty\) the resonators become rigid point masses: only Bragg gaps remain, and the local-resonance gap has moved to infinite frequency.
